# Limitations, possible unfair outcomes and improvements (Phase 9)

This is the written reflection that goes with `docs/evaluation.md`. The numbers below are from that file (seed 42, the
committed data and configuration). It is generated by a script and checked by a test, so if a number here ever disagrees with it,
the generated file is right. Section numbers like "5a" point into it.

**What the evaluation can and cannot show.** I designed both the dataset and the model, so the experiments show how the model
*behaves* (is it consistent, stable, explainable, fair between companies?). They do not show that real travellers would pick the same
offers, because there are no real travellers and no real data. That is the biggest limit of the whole project.

## 1. What held up

- **The rankings behave as the weights say.** Each profile's ranking follows the raw value it favours: the budget ranking follows price
  (rank correlation +0.93), business follows departure time (+0.83), the tourist follows flexibility (+0.70), the environment profile follows
  emissions (+0.84) (1b).
- **The winner is stable.** 15 of 18 weights can move by 10 points or more before the winner changes (2b). Random changes of up to 30%
  to all weights keep the winner in at least 98% of samples for every profile (2c).
- **My own numbers do not decide the result alone.** Changing one scoring parameter at a time, over ranges up to four times either way,
  changed the winner in only 2 of 108 cases (4).
- **A wide price range does not take over.** The budget profile's price influence is 47% both for a EUR 8 price spread (Toledo) and a EUR 38 one (Cadiz) (3b).
- **It is reproducible.** The ranking is identical in repeated runs and for any input order (6).
- **The extension mechanism works.** The fourth profile uses emissions and transfers, which were added as new registered criteria without
  changing the ranking code (D5).

## 2. Limitations and what they affect

| # | Limitation | Evidence | Effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | **Scores for price and duration are relative** to the offers in the list (D15) | 5e: removing one non-winning offer changed the order of the others in 2 of 46 cases, up to 12 pairs swapped. 5b: the winner's score runs from 76.8 to 86.2 as the budget changes, although the offer itself does not | A score only has meaning inside one search, and an offer that is not recommended can still reorder the others. The winner itself was never changed | Use fixed reference scales (e.g. against the traveller's budget) or a hybrid; show the scores as "relative to these offers" (the program already says so) |
| L2 | **The weights are invented** and a weight is not the same as importance | 3a: influence differs from weight by up to +9 points (departure for business) and -10 points (flexibility for the tourist) | A traveller who sets "flexibility 50%" gets less flexibility influence than they asked for, because most offers have the same flexibility | Keep showing weight versus influence; ask the traveller trade-off questions ("EUR 5 or one hour?") and derive the weights; test with real users |
| L3 | **Hard constraints are binary** | 5b: OF-0723 (EUR 33.50) is present at budget 33.50 and completely gone at 33.49 | A traveller just over budget never sees the offer, even if it is much better | Show "near misses" (offers just outside a limit) after the ranking, or turn the budget into a steep penalty zone |
| L4 | **The default preferred time penalises overnight operators** | 5a: night buses score 0 on departure at the default 09:00, against 69 when the traveller prefers 22:00. The best NocheVia offer ranks lower at 09:00 for 3 of 4 profiles | NocheVia is disadvantaged unless the traveller says they like night travel. For the budget profile the price weight outweighs it, so it is not a general effect | Ask for the preferred time (the menu does, but the default is 09:00); score arrival convenience for overnight journeys; do not apply the departure penalty to journeys that are overnight by nature |
| L5 | **Few offers can ever win** | 2d: only 12 of 47 offers win under any weighting, and three offers win 76% of random weightings | The weights choose between a few contenders. The other offers are never shown first, which in a real market would concentrate bookings | Show a "different kind of offer" alongside the winner (for example the best at another profile), as the profile comparison does |
| L6 | **Missing data is punished** | 5f: if VerdeBus stops reporting emissions, its offers in the environment profile's top 5 fall from 5 to 0 (best rank 1 to 36) | This discourages hiding data, but a company with poor data collection is treated like a polluting one | Score "unknown" separately (neutral value plus a visible flag) and show data completeness per company |
| L7 | **The scoring parameters are my choices** | 4: robust one at a time, but only one parameter was changed per test | Combinations of changes were not tested | A joint sensitivity analysis (many parameters changed together), and more values per parameter |
| L8 | **The data is synthetic** (`docs/dataset.md`) | 5d: VerdeBus leads three profiles (15 of 15 destinations for environment, 8 of 15 for business, 7 of 15 for the tourist), SolRuta leads the budget profile (8 of 15) | The "market structure" results mostly show how I generated the companies, not how real companies compete. Ratings are one number per company and route, emissions are estimates, schedules repeat every day, and the dates cover only five days | Real timetables and prices; real review data; a longer calendar |
| L9 | **The explanation uses fixed thresholds** (75 and 40) and compares only with offers that passed the filter (`docs/explanation.md`) | Explanation tests | A criterion at 74 is not called an advantage; the text says nothing about better offers that were filtered out | Thresholds from the data (for example top quartile) and a line about the best removed offer |
| L10 | **Validation checks form, not plausibility** (`docs/validation.md`) | Validation tests | A EUR 1 ticket to Cadiz would pass. A row with two errors reports only the first | Plausible price band per route; report all errors of a row |
| L11 | **Booking has no locking** and identifies a traveller only by name (`docs/booking.md`) | Booking tests | Two programs writing at exactly the same moment could overwrite each other; two people with the same name cannot book the same offer | A database or a lock file; accounts |
| L12 | **Interface** is text only, in English, and reads `07/12/2026` day first (`docs/interface.md`) | Interface tests | Users of other languages or date habits can make mistakes (the formats are in the error message) | A graphical or web interface, translation, ISO dates only |

## 3. Possible unfair outcomes (the report must name them)

1. **Overnight operators** are disadvantaged by the default preferred departure time (L4). It is the traveller's preference that creates it, but the default
   is mine.
2. **Companies with missing or poor data** are punished in the same way as bad performers (L6).
3. **A few offers receive nearly all the attention** (L5), and in the synthetic market one company leads three of four profiles (L8). In a real market this
   would be a concentration effect.
4. **Travellers who fit no profile.** The four profiles are stereotypes ("budget traveller"). A person with another mix must give own weights, which needs some
   understanding of what a weight means (L2).
5. **Travellers just over a limit.** The hard budget and the other hard limits remove offers completely (L3).
6. **Language and names.** The text is English only, and names may contain only letters, spaces, hyphens, apostrophes (straight or curly) and full stops. That
   excludes some names written with other punctuation, in exchange for making sure no card number is ever stored. I think the trade-off is right for this project,
   but it is a trade-off.

## 4. Improvements, in the order I would do them

| Priority | Improvement | Fixes | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Show "near misses" after the ranking | L3, and the "hard limits" in 3.5 | small |
| 2 | Fixed reference scales or a hybrid, and compare with the current model using the same experiments | L1 | medium |
| 3 | Neutral score plus flag for missing data | L6 | small |
| 4 | Ask for the preferred time without a default, and handle overnight journeys | L4 | small |
| 5 | Joint sensitivity analysis | L7 | medium |
| 6 | Elicit weights from trade-off questions, test with a few real users | L2, 3.4 | large |
| 7 | Real data, a database for bookings, a web interface, cancellation and refunds (the optional extensions in the spec) | L8, L11, L12 | large |

## 5. What I learned (for the reflection in the report)

- **Evidence that can be regenerated beats evidence that was copied by hand.** While writing the evaluation I found that a sentence in
  `docs/filtering.md` (a single company left after strict requirements) was false: I had read the first ten lines of a list sorted by departure time and taken
  them for the whole result. The generated experiment (5c) showed the real picture, and the note is corrected in that file. Since then every
  number in the evaluation is produced by a script and checked by a test.
- **Tests found real defects, not only regressions:** a regular expression where `$` also matched a trailing line break (D13), `input` being looked up when the
  function was defined (D21), a generated document that was stale after a wording change (D17/D18), and a name rule that refused the curly apostrophe typed on
  phones (found while writing section 3.6 above). Breaking the code on purpose and checking that a test fails (done for booking and for the menu) was a good test of the tests.
- **A hypothesis is not a finding.** I expected a wide price range to give price more influence, and wrote it as a sentence in the evaluation draft. The experiment
  said the opposite (3b), so the sentence was rewritten. The same happened with the night buses, which are only penalised for some profiles (5a).
- **Design decisions with a cost should be measured, not just defended.** The relative scale (D15) was a deliberate trade-off. The evaluation puts numbers on its cost
  (rank reversal in 4% of cases here, up to 12 swapped pairs) and shows it never changed the winner in these experiments.
